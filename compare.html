Compare and combine

Embeddings, vector search, keyword search, hybrid search, RAG, graph databases, GraphRAG, LangChain, LangGraph: side by side. What each one reads, whether it needs the exact words, when to use one, two or all of them, and which combinations real systems are built from.

They are not rivals. They are five kinds of thing.

Most confusion comes from comparing items that sit on different levels. "RAG or a vector database?" is like asking "a web app or Postgres?": one uses the other. Read the stack from the bottom up: each layer serves the one above it.

The book makes the same point one level up: the RAG pattern can be seen as a special case of an agent, in which the retriever is a tool the model can use.

The mix-ups, settled

Not a choice

RAG or embeddings?

RAG is the pattern; embeddings are one ingredient of its search step. RAG also works with keyword search alone, and no embeddings.

Not a choice

RAG or a vector database?

A vector database is where RAG's vector search keeps its data. One uses the other.

Usually both

Vector search or keyword search?

A real choice, and they fail on different questions. Running both is called hybrid search.

Different questions

Vector database or graph database?

Vector: "which text is about this?" Graph: "what is connected to this?" Many systems need only the first.

An addition

RAG or GraphRAG?

GraphRAG is RAG with a graph as an extra source. It is for relationship questions, not a newer replacement.

Different jobs

LangChain or LangGraph?

LangChain talks to models and tools. LangGraph runs the steps of a flow and saves its state. Use either, both or neither.

Different failures

RAG or finetuning?

RAG when the model lacks facts. Finetuning when it has the facts but the wrong format or behaviour. Chapter 20

Different levels

RAG or an agent?

RAG is one fixed recipe. An agent is a loop in which the model picks its steps, and searching your documents is often one of its tools.

On libraries of this kind, the book's advice is to start building without an orchestration tool. Any external tool adds complexity, and an orchestrator can hide the details of how your system works. Add one when the pipeline has grown enough to need it.

When to use each, and when not

Vector search

People describe what they want in their own words.

They type identifiers, or the data is rows and numbers.

Help centre. "My card got declined abroad" should find the article titled "International payment failures".

Keyword search

The query contains the very term that is in the document.

People paraphrase, or ask in another language.

Logs or a parts catalogue. "E-4012", "M8x40 bolt". A meaning-based search returns every similar-looking code.

Hybrid search

Both kinds of query arrive, which is nearly always true once real users show up.

One search already passes your tests, or two indexes are too much to run.

A shop. "Waterproof jacket for hiking" and "GTX-200 size M" come through the same search box.

Reranker

The right result is found but not on top, or you want to send fewer chunks to the model.

Every millisecond counts, or the first result is already right.

Legal research. Fifty passages mention "termination". The reranker puts the one about termination for cause first.

RAG

Answers must come from your documents, the documents change, and users need the source.

Everything fits in the prompt, or the answer is a number in a database.

Internal assistant. "What is our refund policy?", answered with a link to the policy page.

Graph database

The question is a chain of connections of unknown length.

One or two joins answer it, or the data is prose.

Dependency map. "Which services break if this database goes down?" Org chart. "Who can approve this?"

GraphRAG

The text gives the rule; the graph gives who or what it applies to.

The text alone answers it. Try better search first: far cheaper.

Expenses. "Who must approve my 800 euro claim?" The policy says "a director"; the org chart says which one.

Tool calling

The value is live or personal, or something must be done.

You always need that lookup: call it in code and put the result in the prompt.

Orders. "Where is my parcel?" The answer is in the order system, not in any document.

LangChain

You want to switch model providers by configuration, or use structured output and tools without each provider's format.

One provider and simple calls: its own SDK is less to learn.

Model selection. A team tries three models before choosing, without rewriting the app each time.

LangGraph

The flow branches, loops, waits for a person, or continues a conversation later.

Two calls in a straight line. Write a function.

Insurance claims. Gather facts, draft a decision, wait for an adjuster to approve, then send.

Hands-On Large Language Models · ch. 8, "Dense Retrieval"

The book lists the caveats of vector search from its own experiments: it returns results even when nothing in the archive is relevant, exact phrases are better served by keyword matching, and a model trained on one domain does poorly in another. It points to hybrid search as the answer.

The ladder

Each rung adds machinery. Stay on the lowest one that passes your tests.

  1. Just the model no search at all

    Enough whenThe task needs no outside facts, or the text fits in the prompt: summarising, rewriting, classifying, a short FAQ.

    Climb whenIt answers about facts it cannot know.

  2. One search vector or keyword, inside RAG

    Enough whenA small corpus and one kind of question. Vector for natural questions, keyword for catalogues and codes.

    Climb whenTests show a class of misses: exact terms with vector search, paraphrases with keyword search.

  3. Two searches hybrid, with a reranker

    Enough whenMixed questions over documents. Most document assistants stop here.

    Climb whenAnswers are incomplete although the right text was found.

  4. Search + a graph GraphRAG

    Enough whenAnswers depend on relationships: people, ownership, dependencies.

    Climb whenSome answers are live values or actions.

  5. Search + tools tool calling

    Enough whenThe assistant must look up live data or do things.

    Climb whenDifferent requests need different paths, or a conversation must continue.

  6. All of it, in a flow LangGraph or plain code

    Enough whenA real assistant with several kinds of question, memory and checks.

    ThenStop adding. Keep measuring.

AI Engineering · ch. 7, "Finetuning and RAG" · ch. 6, "RAG"

Two pieces of advice from the book fit this ladder. When you add RAG, begin with basic retrieval such as term-based search: even simple retrieval that brings in relevant knowledge improves answers. And longer context windows will not make RAG obsolete: data keeps growing, models use long context less well, and every extra token costs time and money.

Which pairs make sense

Each card: what the pair does together, and one small case.

Always together

Embeddings + vector database

The embedding model turns each chunk into a vector; the database stores the vectors and finds the nearest ones fast. Neither is useful alone.

Example. 50,000 help articles embedded once and stored in Qdrant; each question is embedded and matched in milliseconds. Under a few thousand chunks, a plain loop does the database's job.

Very common

Vector + keyword search

Their blind spots are opposite. Vector search misses exact terms; keyword search misses paraphrases. Together, the right chunk is almost always among the candidates.

Example. "My laptop was stolen" is found by meaning, "form FIN-140" by its exact code. In this book, both together found the right chunk for 12 of 12 questions.

Add the reranker

Hybrid search + reranker

Fusion merges the two lists but cannot judge between them. A reranker reads the question next to each candidate and puts the best one first.

Example. The two searches disagreed on FIN-140 against FIN-401 and fusion tied them. The reranker picked the right one: 8 of 12 became 12 of 12.

When relationships matter

RAG + a graph

The documents hold the rule; the graph holds who or what the rule applies to. Each alone gives half an answer.

Example. The policy says "a director must approve claims above 500 euros". The org chart says that for Maria, the director is Sara Holm.

Common in assistants

RAG + tools

Retrieval answers what is written down and the same for everyone. Tools fetch what is live or personal, straight from the system that owns it.

Example. "How many holiday days do employees get?" is in the handbook. "How many do I have left?" is a lookup in the HR system.

Optional

LangChain + LangGraph

LangChain wraps each model call: messages, tools, structured output. LangGraph connects the calls into a flow with branches and saved state. Each works without the other.

Example. A support flow: LangGraph routes the question and remembers the conversation; inside each step, LangChain talks to the model.

Only with a reason

Vector database + graph database

Two stores to run and to keep in step with their sources. Worth it when you truly have both kinds of question: about what text says, and about what is connected.

Example. An HR assistant: the handbook in Qdrant, the org chart in Neo4j. A plain document assistant needs only the first.

Rarely

A graph instead of search

A graph stores things and links, not prose. Drop text search and every question whose answer is a sentence in a document goes unanswered.

Example. The graph knows Maria reports to Jonas. It has no idea how long parental leave is.

Small corpora only

A bigger context window instead of retrieval

Sending everything works while it fits and the cost per call is acceptable. As the corpus grows, each call gets slower and dearer, and the one relevant line is buried.

Example. A 10-page FAQ: send it whole. A 200-page handbook: about 133,000 tokens per question, against 1,200 with retrieval.

Use all of them when

  • One assistant faces several kinds of question: policy, personal data, relationships, follow-ups.
  • Each piece was added to fix a failure you measured.
  • Someone will operate it: two or three stores to keep in step with their sources.

Stay with one when

  • The corpus is small and the questions are of one kind.
  • It passes your tests. A second search adds an index, latency and tuning.
  • You have no tests yet. Then you cannot tell whether an addition helped.

Why do graph databases show up so much less?

Reason 1

Most company knowledge is prose

Wiki pages, policies, tickets, emails. Text search reads prose directly. A graph cannot: it needs things and links that somebody has already separated out.

Reason 2

A vector index is nearly free to add

Split, embed, store: an afternoon, with no modelling. A graph needs a schema first (which things? which links?), and then data that fits it.

Reason 3

Someone has to build the graph

Either the structured data already exists (an HR system, a CRM), or a model must extract it from documents, chunk by chunk. That is slow, costs a model call per chunk, and makes mistakes.

Reason 4

Shallow links fit in SQL

"Which team is this person in?" is one join in a database you already run. A graph database earns its place only when chains are long or of unknown length.

Where a graph is the centre

  • Fraud rings, recommendations, social networks.
  • Dependencies: services, packages, supply chains.
  • Who may see what: permissions inherited through groups.
  • Organisation charts and ownership structures.
  • Research fields with dense, curated relationships: drugs and targets, case law and citations.

Where it is the wrong first step

  • A question-answering assistant over documents. Fix the search first.
  • Anything where you would have to invent the graph before having a question that needs it.
  • Small hierarchies a recursive SQL query handles.

Less common does not mean less useful. Where the question is about connections, nothing else answers it: in this book, no amount of better search could name Maria's director (chapter 11). The three source books reflect the same balance: AI Engineering and Hands-On Large Language Models cover vector and keyword search at length, Prompt Engineering for LLMs gives them a few pages, and none has a section on graph databases.
